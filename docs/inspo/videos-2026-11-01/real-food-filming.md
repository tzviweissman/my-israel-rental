# How professionals film food and drink adverts: notes for AI prompts

Tags: [F] = confirmed in a frame I looked at. [N] = narration only. Frames are in SCRATCH/vids/frames/<ID>/.
None of these videos gives a frame-by-frame shot list or exact pour durations. Where I have no number I say so.

## 1. UqB5tmUHCkk, Adorama / Nicole Shapiro, "How to Film Commercial Food Ads" (10:08)
- Shutter 135 degrees, not the film standard 180. [N] and [F]: the director's monitor at 0:15 reads 23.98 fps, 135 deg, ISO 800, 4400 K.
  - Reason: with the camera moving, a commercial wants the product and packaging text sharp, with less motion blur than a drama.
- Lighting layout [N]:
  - Break the light up. Do not use one even 6x8 soft source.
  - Large, soft sources feel pleasing.
  - Use a mirror board to throw a small, tight beam onto the hero object (a salt shaker is the example). It reads about 1 to 1.5 stops over everything else, so the eye goes to the brightest point.
  - Mirrors come in 1, 2 or 3 levels of diffusion, so the light can be hard or soft.
- Colour [N]:
  - Warm light on the food, slightly cooler light on the background layer. "Nothing is completely perfect" gives a feeling of reality.
  - Pick light that flatters the food's own colour. A brown pastry gets warm light to push it.
  - Colour contrast also comes from props: they swapped a beige pan and beige wrap for contrasting colours.
- Camera movement [N]:
  - No handheld on food. The shots are short and must be clean, so the viewer is not fighting to focus.
  - A small robot arm (Motorized Precision "Evo", [F] seen at 3:20 and 6:00) gives truck, jib and arc in one move, plus a top-down shot.
  - Plan the sequence start to finish. They describe: open very close on texture and pull out to reveal; middle shots arc around or jib up as filler that follows the eye; finish with a push-in, possibly rising to a top-down view of the dish.
  - Pick moves that fit the mood. Do not do everything the rig can do.
- Frame check [F]: gobo (cut-out pattern) shadows on the wall at 6:00 and 7:20; a gridded softbox beside the robot; practical lamps in shot; warm sunlit kitchen.
- Discount: gear and workflow chat, not a shot-by-shot. No fps for slow motion given.

## 2. qAa0n-Qxmng, Scott Peters, "Do THIS When Shooting Food" (about 6 min)
- Brief: winter evenings, lots of hard light, long shadows, practical lights in the background for depth, 9:16 vertical. [N]
- Setup [N]; the diagram at 3:20 [F] agrees:
  - Key: Aputure 1200d through a Light Dome 150 (very big softbox), grid on it, strongest diffusion, plus a 4x4 full-diffusion frame with its own grid. It sits to the side of the food, camera at the front.
  - Edge backlight: Aputure 300d with Fresnel lens and barn doors, from behind/side. The diagram shows it at the upper corner pointing across the table.
  - Negative fill: two or three black floppies on the camera-left side, to stop light bouncing off the white wall and cupboards. The diagram shows a black line on the shadow side. Dedolight with gobos to "slash" the back wall.
  - Practicals: fairy lights in the background (dots in diagram).
- Frame rate and shutter [N]:
  - High-speed shots at about 240 and as fast as 480 fps, for "the impact of an egg into flour or the pouring of a liquid".
  - To keep fast things sharp you need a smaller shutter angle or faster shutter, and that needs more light. The 2:20 frame [F] compares 11 deg and 360 deg shutter on a spinning gyroscope: 11 deg sharp, 360 deg blurred.
  - He admits one shot had too wide a shutter for its fps and was slightly soft; he "just about got away with it".
  - At the highest speeds they removed diffusion and grid to gain light, and swapped cheap fairy lights for Aputure B7c bulbs. Reason: the cheap ones appear to flicker on camera at high fps.
- Look: negative fill plus hard key gives deep, moody contrast. Add bounce or fill instead for a lower-contrast look. [N]
- Lens: RED V-Raptor, Pro Blends at f/1.4 on a couple of shots, Hollywood Black Magic filter (strength 1 or less, he is not sure) to take the edge off sharpness. [N]
- Discount: the lighting description is from his gaffer's memory; brand and power numbers matter less than the layout.

## 3. sjvmIEKauGI, Scott Peters, "Secrets Behind Shooting a Drinks Commercial" (12:49, Baileys, three 15 s spots)
Six techniques [N]:
1. Large soft key light. Book light made from three 12K tungsten lamps. Almost shadowless on the table items. "Makes food and drink look beautiful."
2. High frame rate. Mostly 175 fps (lets them ramp speed in the edit), up to 300 fps for liquid macro and ice pours.
3. Hairlight / rim, from behind. Adds a "ping" to the side of the glass and picks out steam from hot coffee as it is poured. [F] at 6:38: a coffee pour into a glass cup, backlit, warm background and wood table. The 7:00 diagram [F] shows the light above and behind the pour, camera in front.
   - The hairlight also touched the hand, so they accepted a bit of hand highlight. Coming over the wall instead would hit the table or flare the lens.
4. Cucoloris (gobo): a 4x4 wooden cut-out in front of a 12K gives a swirly texture on the back wall. Second-setup key was direct 12K through full silk and muslin. A Source Four spotlight was the "steam light". [N]
5. Grip moves for parallax: dolly track-in plus jib down. At extreme slow motion you cannot move fast enough by hand, so to get a real parallax effect "we really needed robotic motion control". Small digital pushes added in post. [N]
6. Practical lights: dimmable lamps and tea lights, night-time bar scene with blackout curtains. Key: Gemini 2x1 through diffusion, a top light on the drink, small Aperture MC lights and tubes behind the bar, a daylight unit as hair/rim on the glass for the pour, others on the curtain and a plant. Flicker on daylight units at high fps was fixed with 300d lights with Fresnels.
- Shot frames [F]: 4:22 top-down milk pour into a slow cooker with props around; 6:38 coffee pour next to a Baileys bottle; 8:40 close-up of nuts falling onto a creamy drink, shallow depth of field, warm gold light.
- Workflow [N]: shoot the fastest high-speed shots first, because they need the most light and setup.
- Discount: the first third is about relationships, rates and crew. Irrelevant to look.

## 4. J0jawPFkaio, Syrp Lab, "How We Shot These 5 Cocktails" (9:09)
Five looks, with lighting diagrams [F at 1:50, 8:40]:
1. Vintage motel: fresnels with barn doors. One light only hits the table, giving a single crisp shadow from the glass. The light is high and moderately far. A second fresnel directly through a cloudy drink gives a warm glow. A small room light catches the top of the ice for a sparkle. A third fresnel lights the panelled background separately for a radial gradient. Orange pleather table for colour pop.
2. Magazine (Bon Appetit style): coral paper, plinth, soft light. Even background gradient by moving the light further back and up (inverse square law). f/13 "because that is how bright our lights are". Low contrast. A glass with a piece of paper stands in for the drink until the last five minutes so it looks fresh.
3. Sci-fi: black background, black Perspex under the glass for a reflection, one source above and behind the subject to rim the glass; clear ice, not acrylic ice, which looks "too perfect and a bit fake". Use a floppy/scrim/matte box to avoid lens flare from the back light. Rotating close-up on a motorised turntable, with a laser level so everything is centred. [F] 3:54: panel above and behind, black flag.
4. Library: warm earthy greens and browns, dark wood bench, window-like soft light, poly board casting a window-sill shadow on the wall, depth of field for a real-room feel, a dimmed practical lamp, rim panel on the glass. Keep light direction consistent.
5. Summer: plastic plant leaves in front of a hard fresnel cast leaf shadows (plant-to-light distance greater than plant-to-subject distance). White bounce on the opposite side. Soft panel on the background to soften shadows. Fresh ice and a top-up of soda water "for a little energy and movement in the bubbles" right before shooting. [F] 8:18: ice-filled glass with a pour, lime and chilli garnish, warm wood table.
- No frame rates stated for any shot. f/13 only on the magazine shot.
- Discount: advert for the Syrp slider and Manfrotto gear; no pour timing.

## 5. PKkULOeYUrM, Cal Thomson, "The Cinematic Lighting Setup Every Food Creator Needs" (3:49)
- 1. Turn the house/overhead lights off. They are yellowish and flatten detail. If you cannot, flag them off with black flags. [N]
- 2. Side or back light the dish; never front light. Frontal light "washes out the food and you lose all detail and texture". Add a big white bounce or fill on the other side to soften shadows. He calls it "filming on the shadow side". [N] [F] 2:20 shows the bounce/fill graphic.
- 3. Light the background separately from the foreground. Soft on the foreground, some "shape" (hard or soft, gobo) on the background. B7C bulbs at about 20 percent for warm background glow. [N]
- Discount: short advice aimed at home recipe videos.

## 6. HuEyrLbJ25w, Vox, "How robots made this food commercial look effortless" (8:29)
Transcript from the English (US) auto captions (the en-orig request had none; one 429, then it worked). About the Jack Daniel's Honey advert: a lime wedge drops into a yellow-lit highball while the camera orbits it in super slow motion.
- Rig [N] and [F] at 3:05 (blueprint labelled RISER): turntable platform with a camera arm about six feet out, camera on a riser, weights on the base, 4 hp motor, 100 RPM max. Camera: Phantom Veo 4K, up to 1,000 fps at 4K raw. The Phantom records to a RAM buffer and keeps the last six seconds; an RC-car servo pressed the trigger.
  - The client wanted parallax (the glass seen from many angles) in slow motion, which means spinning insanely fast, because "once you slow it down you're not moving very fast".
  - Lime dropped by a ceiling release with hinged flaps, preloaded, to hit the drink on cue.
  - A food stylist keeps the drink looking good; "the lighting has to be great."
  - Three six-axis robots for controlled motion of objects.
- Look [F]: 0:28 glass of drink with lime against a saturated yellow set, soft even light (0:36 shows a big honeycomb softbox). 0:40 bottle centred on yellow, one clean ground.
- No shutter angle, final fps, or pour timing stated. The 1,000 fps is the camera's capability, not the speed of the final shot.
- Discount: the sponsor section (Verizon 5G, from about 7:30).

## Across videos: ranked techniques
1. Backlight / rim from behind and above for liquid and steam, with a large soft side key, and negative fill (black) on the opposite side. [2, 3, 4, 5]
2. Slow motion for pours, ice, falling garnishes, splashes: 175 to 300 fps typical, up to 480 for the fastest hits, with a short shutter so the action is crisp. [2, 3, 6]
3. Controlled, slow camera paths: robot or motion control for orbits and pull-outs; no handheld. 135 deg shutter when the camera moves, so packaging stays sharp. [1, 3, 6]
4. Light foreground and background separately. Hard shape or gobo on the wall, soft on the dish. Warm on food, cooler or different on the background. [1, 4, 5]
5. Practical lights (lamps, fairy lights, tea lights) in the background as warm bokeh for depth. [2, 3, 4]
6. Make real things look fresh: clear ice, fresh ice, soda top-up, steam, colour contrast between food and props. [1, 4, 6]
7. Mild diffusion filter to soften the digital edge, but clients may want clean and sharp. [2, 3]
8. Single hero highlight: a tight beam on the hero about 1 to 1.5 stops over the rest. [1]
9. Open on a close texture shot and pull out; end on a push-in. [1]

## Translated for AI prompts
My translation of what the pros said; not tested on a model. (n) = source video number.

Lighting wording:
- "Large soft key light from the side at about 90 degrees to the camera; a narrower light behind and above the glass at about 45 degrees makes a bright rim along the glass edge and lights the steam; the camera side stays dark (black negative fill)." (2, 3, 4)
- "Backlit liquid glows from within; steam bright against a dark background." (3, 4)
- "One hard light, high and a bit far away, gives one crisp long shadow." (4, 2)
- Never ask for front light or an evenly lit flat studio. (5)
- "A separate warm light pool on the back wall with a leaf-shadow or window-shadow pattern; foreground soft." (3, 4, 5)
- "Warm light on the food, slightly cooler tone in the background; props in a colour that contrasts with the food." (1)
- "A small light catching the top of the ice for sparkle." (4)
- Test: does the shadow side of the food stay dark? Reject evenly lit. Does the glass have a thin bright edge? Is steam visible only where backlit?

Speed and motion:
- Pour, splash, ice drop, falling garnish: "slow motion, roughly 175 to 300 fps played at 24 (about 7 to 12 times slower), drops frozen sharp, no streaky blur." (2, 3)
  - The 7 to 12 times figure is my arithmetic (175 to 300 divided by 24). No video gives a pour duration. Judge by eye: crisp droplets, a stream with thickness, folds where it lands.
- Moving camera: "slow smooth orbit or arc on a robot arm, push-in or pull-out reveal; no handheld shake." (1, 6)
- "Sharp product label while the camera moves" (135 deg shutter idea). (1)
- Sequence in one advert: open close on texture and pull out; middle shots arc or rise; end with a push-in or a top-down of the finished dish. (1)
- Test: pause a frame from the fast action. Soft smeared edges on the stream or the garnish = reject. Flickering lamps = reject. (2, 3)

Styling and look:
- "Fresh clear ice with sharp edges, not too regular; bubbles rising after a soda top-up; crisp fresh garnish." (4)
- "Glass on black reflective surface with a reflection underneath, back light above and behind." (4)
- "Slow rotation on a turntable, product exactly centred." (4)
- Warm wood and earth tones for cosy; one saturated colour ground for pop. (3, 4, 6)
- "Slight diffusion glow on the bright rim; shallow depth of field with warm lamp bokeh behind" (2, 3); for an all-sharp macro magazine look, a small aperture (f/13 in the video). (4)
