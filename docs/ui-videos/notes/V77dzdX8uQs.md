## Lessons (concrete, checkable)

1. **Know the buyer in three layers, and put all three on the page.** (1) A line the customer already believes about themselves ("for serious home bakers, not casual ones"), (2) one thing they refuse, said as a rejection ("not a supermarket sheet cake", "no full-coverage look"), (3) one word, name or term only the right customer decodes (an item named after the thing regulars call it). Check: the page's first screen has one belief line, and at least one item or section uses the owner's own insider word. SHOWN 2:04-4:39. **NEW** (3c.2 and 3j ask for specific over generic and the owner's own words, but not the refusal line or the insider name).
2. **Ask the owner two questions that give layer 2.** "What almost stopped customers from buying?" and "What would they be embarrassed to own?" Position the business as the alternative to the second answer. SAID 3:05-3:36. **NEW** (the existing owner questions in 3c cover worries about the product, not the thing the buyer rejects).
3. **A page that says who it is not for sells harder than a page for everyone.** Tracksmith never writes "beginners" or "joggers" and everything reads as serious runners. For our pages: one sentence of who it is for, and nothing that pulls the other way (no "for everyone" line beside it). SHOWN 2:34 slide. **NEW**, but keep it subordinate to the site positioning (never exclude a customer the owner wants).
4. **Proof strength is the opposite of how easy it was to type.** Rank proof when choosing what goes by the button: certificate, licence, named lab or authority, a specific measured claim with a footnote, a named customer with a photo > a star count > "Great product, would buy again". Check: no proof element on a page that someone could write without doing anything. SAID 10:50, SHOWN Coterie 11:22. **ALREADY** (3c.3 "the strongest proof is the hardest to fake") but the ranking and the "costs nothing to type" test are a sharper checklist.
5. **Sort every hesitation into one of three buckets, and have a proof for each.** (a) It works (a measured or tested number, footnoted), (b) people like me use it (real, locally checkable customers), (c) they will deliver (warranty, returns, cancel terms, in plain words). For a small business: (a) a specific figure from the owner ("holds 3 days in the fridge", "gel lasts 3 weeks"), (b) named local customers or places who really buy, (c) one stated policy. SHOWN 11:52-12:53. **NEW** as a structure (3c.3 is "worry beside the button" without the three-bucket frame).
6. **Let the visitor check the proof themselves.** Made In lets you type your city and find a restaurant you have eaten at, with "zero paid endorsements" printed. Small-business version, only if real: "Where you have tasted us" list of real local cafes or events with their names (with their permission), a map or a list by neighbourhood. Check: at least one proof the visitor can verify without trusting the owner. SHOWN 12:00-12:30. **NEW**.
7. **State exclusions and limits plainly, with a vivid specific.** Peak Design's warranty names what it will not cover (poured concrete, hammering tent stakes). "Specific is a tell; vague is what you write to interpret later." Check: the cancellation, refund or guarantee line contains a concrete limit or example, not "terms apply". SHOWN 12:53 (warranty text highlighted). **NEW** (the existing rule asks for the owner's exclusions in their words, line 133 of the rules, but not as a trust device).
8. **Prefer thresholds to markdowns.** A free-delivery threshold plus a returns promise in the announcement bar ("Shipping on us over $150 and 365-day returns") lifts the order total without touching the unit price. SHOWN 6:12. **ALREADY** (3c.7 "free delivery over an amount").
9. **A bundle should change what it is compared with.** "A 13-piece system that replaces 18 pieces" makes $315 weigh against a whole kitchen, not one pan. For us: a catering tray or a gift box compared with the whole table it fills ("feeds 10, with sides"), not with one item. SHOWN 6:12. **ALREADY** in spirit (3c.5 bundles with real saving, 3j "show the deal"); the framing of the comparison is **NEW**.
10. **Send the price-sensitive buyer to a separate shelf.** Peak Design keeps a "PD Certified" refurbished section, lightly discounted, so the main products hold their price. For us: a small "seconds" or "end of day" row on a bakery or gift shop, only when the owner really has it, never on the page's lead product. SHOWN 6:44. **NEW**.
11. **Contain every offer by audience, date or channel.** New-customer offers at email sign-up (Vuori); sales with a printed end date (the mid-year sale hero shows "ends July 15"); win-back and abandoned-cart offers only to one person and only once. Check: every offer has an end date or an audience, and none is always on. SHOWN 7:45-9:17. **ALREADY** (3c.7, 3j.4). The end date printed in a round badge on the hero is a nice visual, **NEW** only as a pattern.
12. **Count the big offers: 2 to 4 a year, and never on the hero product.** Jones Road holds its best seller at one price all year and discounts kits and sets. Check: the lead item never has a "was" price. SAID 9:49, SHOWN Cyber Monday page. **NEW** as a number (3c.7 says "never a discount on the thing they are known for unless they say so").
13. **Name the awkward moment before it happens.** Seed's first box has a booklet that explains the adjustment week; skincare purging, a 3 to 4 week wait, a first wash. Check: the page and the confirmation say what the first days feel like. SHOWN 14:26. **ALREADY** (3c.8, near word for word).
14. **Proof is an answer, not a section.** A block of 30,000 identical five-star one-liners at the bottom answers nothing. Check: no page has a review wall as its only proof; each proof sits beside the price or button it answers. SHOWN 10:20. **ALREADY** (3c.3).
15. **A subscription or repeat plan is a bordered card that states the saving once** (Who Gives a Crap: a boxed "Subscribe" card with "first order" saving and the cadence menu), with pack sizes as three tiles and a "best value" tag only when it is true. SHOWN 8:16. **ALREADY** (3c.5 and 3j.5), shown here as a layout.

## Do not copy

- The "30,000 5-Star Reviews, 4.9 out of 5" wall and any star count or review total: only the owner's real, checkable reviews.
- Invented counters like "4,000+ restaurants, 62 Michelin stars": copy the pattern only with the owner's real, verifiable numbers.
- Any scarcity or countdown. The Mid Year Sale hero is shown only for the real end date; we print a date only when the owner gives one.
- The red "50% OFF" montage and the "you're not premium" scare slide: his sales framing, not a design to follow.
- "Trusted by" logo strip, "free audit", "book a call": his agency pitch (12:53 and 15:57), and the line that you should "sell to rich people" and "attractive people in your ads": do not build copy or targeting around income, it clashes with our positioning (anyone with something to offer or something they need).
- Eight Sleep-style lock-in (a warranty that only holds while a paid membership stays active) is a lock-in tactic, not a trust tactic: do not use.

## For our pages (small-business websites: bakeries, coffee, nail salons, gyms, gift shops, caterers, meat boards)

- Open with one belief line and one thing the owner's customers refuse, in the owner's own words, and name at least one item with the word regulars actually use. That is the "this is for me" moment a premium site gets in the first screen.
- Spend the first fold's trust on a specific, hard-to-fake proof (a named local customer with a photo, a tested figure, a licence) and put one proof by each price and button; no review wall.
- Offers are rare and contained: a printed end date or a first-order audience, a free-delivery threshold in the top bar, never a "was" price on the lead item, and savings shown in money (3j).
- State one plain guarantee and one honest limit ("we will redo it free; we cannot refund a cake after it is cut") beside the button; the limit is what makes the guarantee believable.
- Say what the first days are like (the nail set after a week, reheating, the first gym week) on the page and in the confirmation, before the customer wonders.
- A "where you have tasted us" or "see us near you" block with real named local places turns a claim into something the visitor can check; only with permission and real names.
